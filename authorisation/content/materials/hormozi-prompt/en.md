## Who this guide is for

This guide is written for one very specific reader: you already have something to sell, and you suspect you are selling it poorly. We won't talk to you about motivation or mindset. We will take the sentence you use to describe what you do and rebuild it piece by piece until saying "no" feels more awkward than saying "yes."

It will be directly useful to you if you recognize yourself in one of these cases:

-   **You sell services by the project or by the hour.** Design, marketing, accounting, architecture, translation, development. Every time you send a quote, you get asked, "Why does it cost that much?" and you have no answer that stands on its own.
-   **You run a studio, salon, or center with a physical space.** A hair salon, cosmetology, nails, physiotherapy, a gym, a yoga or Pilates studio, a dental clinic. You compete with the place down the street, and the only lever that comes to mind is lowering the price or giving away the first session.
-   **You consult or guide other people.** Business consulting, mentoring, nutrition, career coaching, therapy, personal training. Your work is intangible, so it is harder to justify its value.
-   **You run online training or sell access to knowledge.** Live workshops, coaching programs, guides, paid communities, templates. You have a lot of content and little clarity about what result you are promising.
-   **You work for yourself and look for clients without a prior relationship.** On freelance platforms, in direct messages, at events. Your profile says the same thing as three hundred other profiles, and you can't stand out.
-   **You sell a physical product with a decent margin and want to package it better.** Supplements, cosmetics, handmade goods, equipment. The product itself is fine, but the offer it is wrapped in is not.

All these cases share one pattern: **the problem is not the work, it is the offer**. You do your job well, your clients are happy, people recommend you, and still every sale is a huge struggle, people haggle over your price, and you get compared with people who do worse work than you. You can't fix that by working harder. You fix it by changing what you promise, how you promise it, and what risk you take on instead of the client.

### Who it is not for

I would rather be honest with you before you invest two hours in this reading:

-   **If you have nothing to sell yet.** We won't be searching for your business idea or niche here. We assume the offer already exists, even if it is weak. If you are at the "I don't know what to sell" stage, start by talking to potential clients rather than polishing an offer that doesn't exist yet.
-   **If you sell at minimal margins and huge volumes.** Reselling electronics, basic food products, trading on aggressive prices. There, logistics and unit cost decide everything, not the offer. You can take some individual ideas from here, but this method is not your main lever.
-   **If you work in a tightly regulated industry and cannot promise results.** Healthcare, legal services, finance in many countries. The guarantee and promise techniques have to be heavily adapted, and sometimes they simply can't be used in their original form. Read the parts on clarity and on reducing effort, and skip the part on aggressive guarantees.
-   **If you are looking for magic formulas or income figures.** You will not find promises of how much you will earn here. You will find a way to think about your offer and write it down. What happens next depends on your market, your product, and how many people hear you.
-   **If you expect AI to do everything on its own.** The prompts in this guide are excellent at evaluating and suggesting. But they don't know your clients. If you give AI a vague description, it will hand you back a vague offer, very sure of itself.

## What problem it solves, in one sentence

> **If people are unclear about the result, the terms, or the amount of their own work, that is worth checking in the text of your offer and in conversations with clients. This guide gives you a way to run that check and rewrite the text.**

When someone says "people aren't buying from me," it almost always means one of three things: it isn't clear what exactly the client gets; the client doesn't believe they will achieve it; or it seems to them that it will take too much work and too much time. None of the three is fixed by a better page design or extra advertising. It is fixed in the text of the offer itself.

## What you will be able to do when you finish reading

This is not reading for general education. When you finish, you should have four concrete things in hand:

1.  **Your offer written as one sentence that is clear the first time.** With the result, the timeframe, the audience, and what sets you apart. No filler adjectives. A sentence you can say out loud to a stranger, and they can repeat it to someone else without error.
2.  **A price you know how to defend.** Not a number taken from peeking at competitors, but one you can justify in three sentences, because you know the value of what you take off the client's shoulders and the risk you take on.
3.  **A rewritten description of your service.** The one on your website, in your profile, in the quote you send, in the cold message you send out. They all say the same thing, and they have all stopped talking about you and started talking about the client.
4.  **A ready answer to "that's expensive."** Not a discount. An answer that brings the conversation back to value, and it works because your offer is already built to withstand it.

And one more thing, less obvious but more useful in the long run: you will stop seeing text in your offer and start seeing four numbers that you can raise or lower at will. That turns "I don't know what to do to sell more" into "this week I'm working on this lever."

## Who is Alex Hormozi, and why do people talk so much about his prompts?

Alex Hormozi is an entrepreneur and the author of the books "$100M Offers" and "$100M Leads." In this guide we break down his publicly described value formula as a working model for analyzing an offer. This does not replace customer research and does not mean the author took part in creating the guide.

What made him known is not motivation but exactly the opposite: **he turns complex topics into formulas you can apply the same day**. Instead of "believe in yourself," he gives you an equation, a checklist, and an example. That is why his ideas translate so well into prompts: they already exist as criteria, and AI knows how to work with criteria.

His central concept is the **irresistible offer**: an offer so clear and so valuable that saying "no" costs more than saying "yes." It sounds simple, and that is exactly its strength: it forces you to answer uncomfortable questions. What result do you promise, in what timeframe, what happens if the result doesn't arrive, and why should anyone believe you at all?

**Where it works best:** services, agencies, online training, fitness and health, B2B, any business where someone sells a result. **Where to be careful:** very low-margin goods, regulated markets, and anything that doesn't depend on direct sales. He is not a prophet: he is someone who is very good at packaging offers and attracting clients. Treat him that way and he will be useful to you.

## The value equation, element by element

The whole methodology fits into a single fraction. On top are two things you want to **maximize**. On the bottom are two things you want to **minimize**. The result is the value perceived by the person reading your text, which is not the same as the real value of your work, but it alone determines whether they buy.

Outcome: clearer · Trust in the outcome: higher · Wait: shorter · Client effort: lower

Four levers. The two on top should be raised, the two on the bottom lowered. Most businesses touch only the first and forget the other three, even though those are usually easier to move.

Before going through the elements one by one, remember this idea, because it changes your whole approach: **the fraction moves much faster through the bottom than through the top**. Promising twice the result means multiplying by two. Halving the perceived effort is also multiplying by two, and it usually looks more convincing, is cheaper to carry out, and is harder to copy. When a business says, "we raised our price and started selling more," it almost always actually worked on the denominator.

### 1\. Dream outcome: what the client actually gets

**What it is:** the state your client wants to reach, described in their words, not yours. Be careful here, because this is where almost everyone stumbles. You sell your method; they buy what comes "after." Nobody wants twelve physiotherapy sessions. People want to bend down and tie their shoes without wincing. Nobody wants an accounting audit. People want to sleep peacefully when an inspection comes.

**How to raise it:**

-   **Go one level above what you deliver.** Ask yourself three times in a row, "and why does he want that?" until you reach something that can be seen, counted, or felt. "I'll build you a website" → "you have a website" → "people find you" → "you receive quote requests without chasing anyone." That last one is the result.
-   **Name a specific situation, not a category.** "Improve your image" is not a result. "Go to your sister's wedding and feel good in the dress you've already bought" is a result.
-   **Add an emotional result next to the practical one.** The practical one justifies the purchase to other people; the emotional one is what triggers it. "The books are in order" is practical. "Stop opening your mail with dread" is emotional. Both fit in one sentence.
-   **Listen to how clients themselves put it.** The best offer phrases aren't written, they are transcribed. Take your last ten sales conversations, your reviews, and thank-you messages, and underline the verbatim expressions people use. Those go into the text.

**Examples by industry:**

-   *Beauty salon:* not "a coloring service," but "color that lasts eight weeks, and regrowth doesn't show in photos."
-   *B2B consulting:* not "process optimization," but "your team stops spending Fridays manually reconciling reports."
-   *Online training:* not "learn to use AI," but "finish by six what used to keep you until nine."
-   *Pilates studio:* not "training your core muscles," but "a back that holds up through eight hours in the office and doesn't hurt."
-   *Freelance graphic design:* not "visual identity," but "so your brand stops looking smaller than it is."

### 2\. Perceived likelihood of achievement: why they will believe you

**What it is:** how much your client believes that this result will happen to *them*. Not to others, not in theory, but to them, with their situation, their lack of time, and their past failed attempts. This is the most underrated of the four elements. You can have the most desirable promise on the market and zero sales if nobody believes it will work.

**How to raise it:**

-   **Specific proof instead of adjectives.** One before-and-after, one number, one case with a name and context is worth more than ten "we are experts." And context means "similar to their case": if your client is a neighborhood hair salon, a case study of a chain with twenty locations doesn't convince them, it pushes them away.
-   **Explain the mechanism.** People believe more in what they understand. "This works because we do X, then Y, and that's why Z happens" turns a promise into a process. You don't need to reveal the whole method: it is enough to show that there is a system behind it, not improvisation.
-   **Take on the risk.** Every condition you remove from the client's side raises this score. A money-back guarantee, a no-commitment first stage, payment after delivery, "we continue until it works." Be careful: the guarantee must be plausible, and you must be able to honor it, or it backfires.
-   **Lower the promise until it becomes believable.** It sounds contradictory, but it works. A moderate result that people believe sells more than a huge one they don't. If your promise sounds like a miracle, trim it, and you will gain more in trust than you lose in desire.
-   **Name the exceptions yourself.** "This won't work if you can't spend two hours a week on it" is a sentence that raises trust in everything else. Someone who sets limits on their own promise looks like a person who knows what they are talking about.

**Examples by industry:** a dental clinic that shows real X-rays of treated cases; a consultant who, with the client's permission, shows their dashboard with the data hidden; a nail technician with photos from three weeks later, not only from the day of the visit; an agency that also publishes the projects where the result took longer than planned and explains why.

### 3\. Time delay: when it starts to be felt

**What it is:** the gap between payment and the first noticeable change. Not between payment and completion of the work, but between payment and the moment when *something becomes visible*. That is a huge difference. If your service delivers results in six months, but the client feels a real shift on day three, your perceived time delay is three days, not six months.

**How to reduce it:**

-   **Design a deliberate quick win.** Something small, useful, and visible in the first 48 hours. A completed diagnostic, a template that already saves work, a change with a measurable effect. This isn't a gift: it is part of the product, deliberately placed at the very start.
-   **Break the path into dated milestones.** "In twelve weeks" is frightening. "Week 1: diagnostic, week 2: first change launched, week 4: first data" is reassuring, even if the total timeframe is the same.
-   **Start working before the first meeting.** If the client pays on Monday and the first session is on Thursday, those three days are pure waiting. Send something that same Monday.
-   **Make invisible progress visible.** A lot of work moves forward unnoticed by the client. A short status message, a screenshot, a brief weekly report costs little and cuts the feeling of waiting enormously.
-   **Name dates, not vague ranges.** "As soon as possible" feels like a longer wait than "the 14th." Always.

**Examples by industry:** a gym that takes measurements at the first visit and hands over a printed plan the same day; a consulting firm that sends a summary of three identified risks within 24 hours; an agency that launches the first campaign in 72 hours, even if the full strategy will be ready in three weeks; a training specialist who opens access to the materials at the moment of payment, not on the start date.

### 4\. Effort and sacrifice: how much work is left for the client

**What it is:** everything the client has to do, learn, decide, give up, and endure to reach the result. This includes the obvious (time, discipline) and what almost nobody talks about: the decisions they have to make, the tools they have to learn, the things they have to find and send you, social awkwardness, the fear of doing something wrong.

**How to reduce it:**

-   **Move work from their side to yours.** This is the most powerful of the four levers, and almost nobody uses it, because it costs money and real effort. But that is exactly why it works: nobody will copy it from you in one evening. If you ask the client for ten pieces of data, get eight yourself and ask for two.
-   **Remove decisions.** Every option you give them is a chance to get stuck. Three well-thought-out plans sell more than twelve combinations. Offer one default option and explain why you recommend it.
-   **Remove friction at entry.** Long questionnaires, mandatory calls before the client learns the price, complicated sign-ups. Every extra step before the start is perceived effort, and you pay for it in lost sales.
-   **Hand over finished work, not just explanations.** Filled-in templates, ready examples, settings already assembled. The difference between "I'll explain how" and "here it is done, check it" is gigantic in how effort is perceived.
-   **Remove what causes shame or fear.** Some effort is not about time but about emotion: standing in front of a camera, calling a long-time client, admitting that something was done wrong. If your offer softens this, say so directly.
-   **Ban "all you need to do is" from your vocabulary.** Every time you write "all you need to do is," you shift work to the client and disguise it as ease. Replace it with "we do this for you" whenever you can afford to.

**Examples by industry:** an aesthetic cosmetology center that parks the client's car and takes and returns her coat; a consulting company that fills in the intake questionnaire itself from public data and asks only for confirmation; an accounting service that picks up invoices from a shared folder instead of asking them to be emailed; a coaching program that hands over texts already written so the client only has to approve them.

#### A five-minute exercise

Take your current offer and rate each element from 1 to 10: how desirable the result is, how believable it is, how fast the first noticeable changes come, how easy it is for the client. Don't deliberate: the first score is the right one. Now look at the lowest: that is your work for this week. Most people discover that their lowest score is not the result but the effort or the delay. And those two elements can be fixed in days, not months.

## Four steps: from your current offer to a testable new version

Diagnose the current offer · Check the four value elements · Rewrite the offer · Test on real people

Four steps, in exactly this order. Skipping the first is the most common mistake: you end up polishing the wording of an offer that was built wrongly at its foundation.

### Step 1 · Break your current offer into parts

Don't start by writing. Start by copying. Open a blank document and paste in, as is, without editing anything, the text you use to sell today: the description from your website, the message you send in private conversations, what you say on the first call. If you have never written it down, write it now the way you would say it out loud. No embellishing: the goal is to see your real starting point.

Now answer these six questions in writing, in full sentences:

1.  What exactly does the client get when we finish the work? (An object, a state, a number.)
2.  How much time passes before they notice the first effect? And before they get the full result?
3.  Why should they believe me? What proof do I have, from their own industry or situation?
4.  What does the client have to do? List everything, including the small and unpleasant things.
5.  What happens if it doesn't work? Who loses, them or me?
6.  How am I different from the next person who does the same thing? Without using the words "quality" and "individual approach."

If any answer turns out long, tangled, or uncomfortable, don't fix it, mark it. The uncomfortable spots are your working material. The worst ones are almost always the answers to questions 4 and 5, and they almost always cost you the most in sales.

#### Warning sign

If, when answering "what does the client get," you find yourself listing what you do ("sessions, reports, support"), your offer is described from the inside. This is the most common diagnosis, and the easiest to fix.

### Step 2 · Strengthen each element of the formula

With the diagnosis in front of you, go element by element. One element per work session, without mixing them, and write the options down roughly, without evaluating them yet. This is where AI comes in: the two mega-prompts in the next section are built for exactly this, and they work much better if you come to them with step 1 already done.

The recommended order, and it is not accidental:

1.  **Effort first.** This is what raises value the most and depends the least on writing skill. Find three things the client does today that you could do. Calculate what it would cost you. Keep the cheapest of the three.
2.  **Then the delay.** Design the first-48-hours win. It must be something real, not a welcome message.
3.  **Likelihood comes third.** Collect two concrete proofs and describe the mechanism in three sentences. Decide what risk you can take on without going broke.
4.  **The outcome comes last.** Now write the promise. We deliberately leave it for last: once you have already cut the effort and the delay, the promise practically writes itself and sounds more convincing too, because the whole rest of the offer backs it up.

When you finish, you should have a rough list of twelve or fifteen ideas. It's fine if half of them are unfeasible. Cross out the ones you can't deliver today (delivering what you promise is not up for debate) and keep four or five.

### Step 3 · Rewrite the whole offer

Now everything comes together in one text. The structure that works best, which you can copy word for word, is in the templates section. Three rules while you write:

-   **One sentence, one idea.** If a sentence needs two commas and one "moreover," split it.
-   **Numbers wherever possible.** "Fast" means nothing. "Within 72 hours" does. "Many clients" doesn't. "Nineteen hair salons" does.
-   **Zero filler words.** Innovative, comprehensive, holistic, synergy, quality, excellence, tailored. Cross them all out. If the phrase loses its meaning after you do, it had none to begin with.

When the text is ready, read it out loud. It sounds strange, but it is the fastest check there is: any fragment you stumble over while reading is a fragment where the client gets lost. Then run a tough test: show the text to a person who knows nothing about your industry and ask them to explain in their own words what you sell and to whom. If they can't, it isn't that they don't understand; the text just isn't ready yet.

### Step 4 · Test the offer on real people

An offer isn't tested in a document. It is tested in conversations. And you don't need to wait for traffic or an advertising budget:

-   **Five sales conversations with the new version.** Changing nothing from one conversation to the next. Write down word for word which phrase gets you interrupted, what they ask, where they go quiet.
-   **Write to five former clients.** "I'm rethinking how I present my service. Would you tell me what you think of it and what's unnecessary here?" They reply more often than you think, and they answer better than any stranger.
-   **Publish a short phrase where people see you.** A profile, a status, a post. See whether anyone asks. Silence is information too.
-   **Change one thing at a time.** If you change the promise, the price, and the guarantee all at once and things get better, you won't know what exactly worked. And you won't be able to repeat it.

The sign that you're on the right track is not the words "how beautiful." It is that people ask you **"so how much does it cost?"** before you bring up the price yourself, or **"when could we start?"** When those two questions show up on their own, the offer is doing its job.

## ⚙️ Mega-prompt for evaluating the Dream Score

**This Dream Score mega-prompt calculates the value of your offer across 4 parameters:**

**Dream Outcome** — what is the BEST possible result this product can deliver?
**Perceived Likelihood of Achievement** — how likely is it that the buyer will reach the dream outcome?
**Time Delay** — how long will it take to reach the dream outcome?
**Effort and Sacrifice** — how much effort must the buyer put in to reach the dream outcome?

To create irresistible offers, you need to maximize the dream outcome and the likelihood of achieving it, and minimize the time delay, effort, and sacrifice.

**How to get the most out of it:** into `[DESCRIBE YOUR OFFER]`, paste everything you wrote in step 1, not a short summary. State who you sell to, what the service includes, how much it costs, how long it lasts, what guarantee you offer today, and which objections you usually hear. A three-line description will give you a three-line analysis. A twenty-line description will give you something you can actually use.

```
#ROLE:
Analyze my offer using the value formula described by Alex Hormozi. Do not pretend to be the author and do not make up data.

#INFORMATION ABOUT ME:
- For context: my offer is as follows:
[DESCRIBE YOUR OFFER]

#HOW YOU SHOULD RESPOND:
Evaluate my offer using Alex Hormozi's value equation, which has 4 parts:

1) How desirable is this offer's dream outcome on a scale from 1 to 100? ("Dream Score")
2) How high is the perceived likelihood of achieving this outcome on a scale from 1 to 100? ("Success Score")
3) How long is the perceived wait between purchase and the promised outcome on a scale from 0 to 1? ("Time Score") The longer the wait, the higher the score. Ideally, the perceived wait should be as low as possible.
4) How large are the perceived effort and sacrifice on a scale from 0 to 1? ("Effort Score") The greater the perceived effort, the higher the score. Ideally, the perceived effort and sacrifice should be as low as possible.

After scoring all 4 components, calculate the final offer score ("offer score") like this:

1) Multiply "Dream Score" by "Success Score"
2) Multiply "Time Score" by "Effort Score"
3) Divide the result of the first product by the result of the second, and you get the "offer score"

#OUTPUT:
In your answer, give me specific, practical advice on how to adjust my offer to raise the score on each of the 4 components of the value equation.

In addition, suggest 2 more offer structures with a higher score that I could use instead of my current one, and explain why they score higher.
```

The final score in the prompt is a teaching heuristic for comparing two versions of the same offer, not a measurement of the probability of sales. Don't allow zero in the denominator, and test the AI's ideas on real clients.

The terms are deliberately kept in their original form so any model recognizes them best. In plain words: *Dream Score* is how desirable the outcome is; *Success Score* is how much people believe it will be achieved; *Time Score* is how long the wait feels; *Effort Score* is how much work is left to the client. The last two are measured from 0 to 1 and sit in the denominator: the lower they are, the better.

**How to read what it gives back.** The final number means nothing by itself, and there is no such thing as a "good" score. What matters is something else: **which of the four components turned out worst** and what happens to the total when you change one of them. Save the score of your current version, apply the changes, and run the same prompt on the new version. The useful information is precisely that comparison.

## Improve your offer (use after the first prompt)

This second prompt runs **in the same conversation**, right after the AI has scored you. It asks for something deliberately exaggerated: to push the offer to an impossible score. And it works for exactly that reason: when you push the AI to an extreme, it stops suggesting cosmetic tweaks and starts suggesting structural changes.

```
Adjust this offer as much as needed until it reaches a perfect score of 1,000,000

(Dream Score = 100, Success Score = 100, Time Score = 0.1, Effort Score = 0.1)
```

What it gives back will almost certainly be impossible to deliver in its original form. Don't copy it. Use it as a list of extreme ideas and ask yourself one single question about each: **"which version of this can I actually deliver starting Monday?"** If the AI suggests "a guaranteed result in 24 hours or a full refund," perhaps your real version is "a diagnostic within 24 hours, and if what you see doesn't suit you, we don't continue and you don't pay for the second stage." Less spectacular, but completely deliverable, and it improves all four metrics just the same.

#### Three extra questions worth a lot

After the two prompts, keep pulling the thread in the same conversation:

-   "Of everything you suggested, which three changes would cost me the least and raise the score the most?"
-   "Write me the five most likely client objections to this offer and a short answer to each."
-   "Rewrite the offer in one sentence, with no adjectives, the way a happy client would tell a friend about it."

The last one usually gives the best phrase of the whole session.

## Before and after: six rewritten offers

The six examples below are teaching scenarios, not real client results. Timeframes, refund terms, and other specific promises are given only to break down the structure: use them in your own offer only if you can truly deliver.

Before: a general promise with no timeframe or proof · After: a specific result, timeframe, terms, and a way to verify

The same person, the same work, the same price. Only what is promised changes, along with the timeframe, the proof, and how much work is left to the client.

None of these examples invents a new service. In all six cases the specialist does exactly what they did before; only the offer changes. Read them looking for the pattern, not the specific wording: your field will be different, the mechanism is the same.

1 · Hair salon and coloring

WeakProfessional coloring with top-quality products and personal consultations. Book an appointment!

StrongColor that lasts eight weeks with no visible regrowth. We will send you a photo of how it will look before we touch your hair, and if after three weeks the result is not what we agreed, we will correct it for free.

**Why this works:** the first version is interchangeable with any salon in the country. The second sets a measurable result (eight weeks), removes the client's main fear (that it won't turn out as she expected) by showing the result before the work begins, and shifts the risk onto the salon. The price may be the same; the decision no longer is.

2 · Bookkeeping support for the self-employed

WeakComprehensive accounting and tax services. Over 15 years of experience. Trust the best professionals.

StrongWe handle your taxes without you sending us anything: we connect to your bank and your invoicing system, and you only confirm a one-page summary once a quarter. If a notice arrives from the tax office, we respond to it.

**Why this works:** the self-employed person's real pain is not the bookkeeping but having to gather and forward papers. The second version hits the whole denominator at once: it removes effort (you send nothing), removes decisions (you confirm one page), and removes fear (we answer the notice). "15 years of experience" says none of this.

3 · B2B marketing consulting

WeakA 360º digital marketing strategy for your business growth. Audit, plan, and implementation.

StrongIn 14 days we hand you a client acquisition plan your team can carry out without us, with three priority actions and materials already written. The first week is a diagnostic: if, after seeing its results, you decide this isn't right for you, you stop there and pay only for that stage.

**Why this works:** "360º" and "growth" can't even be pictured. The second version sets a timeframe (14 days), a specific result (plan + written materials), and breaks consulting's main brake, the fear of signing a long contract with a stranger, by splitting the commitment into two parts. It reduces the delay, reduces the effort, and raises trust all at once.

4 · Pilates studio

WeakImprove your posture, strength, and well-being with our method. The first trial class is free.

StrongFor people who spend eight hours sitting and end the day with a stiff back. Groups of six, two fixed days a week that you choose at the start and don't change. After six weeks we repeat the mobility assessment from day one, and you compare both results.

**Why this works:** the first version addresses everyone, which means no one. The second names a recognizable situation, removes the weekly decision of "which day do I go," which can get in the way of attending regularly, and turns a vague promise of well-being into a comparable measurement. A trial class on its own doesn't explain what the person will keep paying for.

5 · Freelance graphic design

WeakBrand identity design. Logo, brand book, and collateral. Free, no-obligation quote.

StrongA brand ready to use in 10 days: a logo, colors, fonts, and the 12 templates you will actually need (posts, a proposal, an invoice, a presentation), already laid out and editable. Two rounds of revisions are included, and a third too if the mistake is mine.

**Why this works:** a brand book in PDF is a document the client doesn't know how to use; twelve templates already laid out are work they no longer have to do. On top of that, the timeframe is fixed and the revision policy is stated in advance, which in this field is a usual source of conflict and distrust.

6 · Online coaching program

WeakLearn to use artificial intelligence in your business. Over 40 videos, a private community, and lifetime updates.

StrongOn the very first day you automate a specific task from your week and see how much time it saves you. After six weeks you will have five tasks crossed off your schedule, with templates already set up for your business. If you haven't automated anything by the end of week 1, we refund your money.

**Why this works:** "40 videos" is effort disguised as value: the more hours of content, the more work for the buyer. The second version moves the result to day one, counts the benefit in tasks removed from the schedule rather than material consumed, and ties the guarantee to something verifiable within seven days, instead of the standard 30-day refund nobody believes in.

#### The pattern in all six

Notice what repeats: **adjectives disappear and numbers appear**; the subject becomes the client instead of the specialist; there is always a timeframe; there is always something the client no longer has to do; and in five cases out of six someone takes on the risk, and that someone is the seller.

## Templates and formulas to copy

Everything that follows is designed so you can copy it and fill it in. Replace the words in square brackets with your own and keep only what you can actually deliver. An unfulfilled promise does more harm than a modest one.

### Offer framework

```
FOR [exactly who this is, with a description of their situation]
WHO [the specific problem or frustration they live with today]

YOU WILL GET [the result, phrased in the client's words]
IN [timeframe]

WITHOUT [what people usually have to suffer through, but not here]

THIS WORKS BECAUSE [the mechanism in one sentence]
PROOF: [a case, a number, or a demonstration]

INCLUDES:
- [component 1, phrased as a result]
- [component 2]
- [component 3]

ALL WE NEED FROM YOU IS [the minimum, one or two actions]
WE TAKE CARE OF [everything else, list it]

IF NOT [verifiable condition], THEN [what happens: refund, we continue for free, you don't pay for the next stage]

INVESTMENT: [price] · [payment format]
```

When you've filled in the template, compress it into one sentence. That short phrase goes in the headline, in the profile, and in the first line of the message; the long block goes below, for those who want the details.

### Formulas for the short phrase

```
I help [whom] achieve [result] in [timeframe] without [the usual sacrifice].

[Specific result] in [timeframe], or [consequence if it doesn't happen].

For [whom] who already tried [alternative that didn't work] and stayed in the same place.

We do [the heavy part]; you only [the minimal part].

From [current state, named without sugarcoating] to [desired state] in [timeframe].
```

### Guarantee wording

A guarantee is not only a refund. There are four families of guarantees, ordered from the mildest to the most aggressive. Choose the strongest one you can sustain without putting your business at risk:

```
CONDITIONAL (the easiest to deliver)
"If you follow the agreed plan and haven't achieved [verifiable
result] within [timeframe], we keep working for free until you do."

RESULT GUARANTEE
"If you haven't [measurable result] within [timeframe], we refund you [amount]."

PROCESS GUARANTEE
"If within [short timeframe] you don't have [specific deliverable] in hand,
you don't pay for this stage."

REVERSE (you prove it first)
"We complete the first stage and show it to you. If what you see doesn't suit you,
you stop there and pay nothing."
```

Two rules apply to all four: tie the guarantee to something **verifiable** (a date, a specific deliverable, a number, and never the wording "if you're not satisfied," which can be disputed) and set **honest terms, stated in advance**. A guarantee with hidden fine print destroys more trust than it creates.

### Ways to remove risk without giving a guarantee

-   **Split the commitment into parts.** A short, inexpensive first stage, the decision to continue later. This lowers the entry threshold without lowering the total price.
-   **Payment at the end, or in installments tied to checkpoints.** A percentage at the start and the rest when the deliverable is approved.
-   **Show the process before the sale.** A real example of how you work, an excerpt from a report, a recording of a working session made with permission.
-   **Let them leave without penalty.** Cancellation at any time, no required term. It sells more than it costs.
-   **Set limits yourself.** "We don't work with \[case\] because we don't get results there." A public filter raises trust in everything else.

### Bonuses that genuinely add value

A bonus is not just some gift: it exists to remove the client's **next** obstacle, the one that appears right after your service. If your bonus doesn't remove an obstacle, it's noise.

-   **The one that saves work:** an already filled-in template, a ready setup, a list of vetted suppliers.
-   **The one that speeds things up:** immediate access to something useful from the first minute, before everything else even begins.
-   **The one that gives confidence:** a check-in after 30 days, a direct channel for questions during the month, a review session.
-   **The one that answers "what next":** a guide to maintaining the result, an action plan for the next month, materials so the team can carry on.
-   **The one that prevents an expensive mistake:** a list of the ten most common mistakes in the client's situation and ways to avoid them.

A practical rule: **three well-chosen bonuses are stronger than eight.** A long list of gifts reads as filler and lowers the perception of the main product.

### Ready-made phrases for talking about price

```
When they ask about price too early:
"I'll tell you in a moment, but the price depends on [factor]. Tell me [short question],
and in a minute I'll give you the exact figure."

When they say "that's expensive":
"I understand. Expensive compared to what: another offer, or not doing this at all?"

When they compare you with someone cheaper:
"Yes, there are cheaper options. The difference is that there you do [the work that is left
to the client] yourself, and here we do it. If you have the time for that,
the cheaper option will suit you just fine."

When they ask for a discount:
"I can't lower the price, but I can adjust the scope: we remove
[part], and it comes to [amount]. Does that suit you better?"

When they say "I need to think about it":
"Of course. Which part exactly isn't convincing you yet: the result,
the timeframe, or the price?"

To justify the price without apologizing:
"It costs [price] because it includes [what we take off your shoulders]. If
you did it yourself, it would take [time], and you would still have to [redo/check it]."
```

The principle behind all these phrases: **never answer a price objection with a discount**. A discount confirms that the price was inflated and trains your client to haggle every time. Answer with an adjusted scope, an honest comparison, or a question.

## Five mistakes that sink a good offer

### Mistake 1 · Promising more instead of taking work off the client

**What happens:** sales drop, and the automatic reaction is to raise the promise. From "we'll improve your online presence" to "we'll double your visibility." The promise grows, trust falls, and the client still has to do exactly the same work as before.

**Why it doesn't work:** in the equation, growth of the numerator has a ceiling: the limit of what people are willing to believe. Reducing the denominator has no ceiling, or almost none. Besides, the work taken off the client can be checked immediately, while a loud promise can be checked only months later.

**How to fix it:** go back to your list from step 1, the inventory of everything the client does. Pick the heaviest item and take it on yourself. Then, instead of raising the promise, write what the client no longer has to do. This is harder to copy and much easier to believe.

### Mistake 2 · A result that can't be pictured

**What happens:** "we help you grow," "we improve your well-being," "we develop your business." Phrases that produce no picture in the reader's head.

**Why it doesn't work:** people buy not concepts but scenes. If a person can't see themselves in a specific situation after working with you, they have nothing to compare your price with, and then any price seems high.

**How to fix it:** run the "video test." If you were filming the client on the day they get the result, what would be visible on screen? What would they be doing, saying, no longer doing? That scene, written in one sentence, is your result. "Growing" can't be seen on screen. "Turning down a project because the schedule is already full" can.

### Mistake 3 · Not saying when

**What happens:** the offer describes perfectly what the client will get and says nothing at all about when. Or worse: it says "in a short time," "quickly," "soon."

**Why it doesn't work:** when there is no timeframe, the client invents one, and always invents a worse one than the real one. The absence of a date reads not as flexibility but as "this will drag on, and they don't want to tell me."

**How to fix it:** give two dates, not one. The date of the first visible effect and the date of the full result. "First report in 48 hours, a fully working system in six weeks." If you are afraid to commit, start with the date of the first interim result, which depends only on you.

### Mistake 4 · A guarantee nobody believes

**What happens:** "100% satisfaction guarantee," "guaranteed refund," "guaranteed results." Words that have been used so often they no longer mean anything.

**Why it doesn't work:** a guarantee without specific conditions reads as a legal formula, not a commitment. And an experienced client knows that "if you're not satisfied" is a condition that cannot be proven, and assumes in advance that such a guarantee will be impossible to use.

**How to fix it:** make the guarantee specific, verifiable, and a little uncomfortable for you. "If the report isn't in your inbox by March 15, you don't pay for this stage" is a guarantee that is modest on paper and extremely powerful in practice, because it is concrete and can be checked without arguments. And phrase it in your own words, not in legal language.

### Mistake 5 · Cutting the price instead of raising the value

**What happens:** clients aren't coming, so a promotion launches. Some come, and when the promotion ends, sales stall again. Then it all repeats. A year later, the price benchmark in the market is the price of your promotions.

**Why it doesn't work:** a discount touches none of the four elements. It makes the result neither more desirable, nor more believable, nor faster, nor easier. It merely buys temporary attention, and attracts precisely the client who demands the most and values the least.

**How to fix it:** before touching the price, spend the same money on the denominator. If you were going to give a 20% discount, calculate how much that is in euros, and invest that amount in taking on part of the client's work, speeding up delivery, or adding a bonus that removes their next obstacle. The same cost to you, a completely different effect, and meanwhile you aren't training anyone to wait for a sale.

#### The sixth mistake, just in case

Changing everything at once and writing nothing down. If on Monday you rewrite the whole offer and the following month goes better, you won't know what exactly worked, and you won't be able to repeat it in your next product. Save the previous version, write down the date of the change and what exactly you changed. It takes two minutes and saves a year of wandering blind.

## Checklist: go through it with your offer before selling

Read it with your new offer in front of you, and check off only what you can answer with a confident "yes." The unchecked items are your task list.

### Outcome

-   Is the result phrased in the client's words, not yours?
-   Can it be pictured as a concrete scene, not as a concept?
-   Does the promise include a number, an object, or a verifiable state?
-   Can you say what the client stops doing once they achieve the result?
-   Have you removed all the filler adjectives: comprehensive, professional, personalized, high-quality?

### Credibility

-   Do you explain why this can be believed: data, case studies, a guarantee, a demonstration?
-   Do you have at least one piece of confirmation from someone similar to your target client?
-   Can you explain the mechanism in three sentences, without special terms?
-   Do you say out loud who this is not for?
-   Does your promise sound convincing to a skeptic, or does it sound like a miracle?

### Time

-   Is it clear how long it takes for this result to arrive?
-   Does the client get something useful in the first 48 hours?
-   Is the path broken into stages with dates, or is it one solid block?
-   Do you name specific dates instead of "as soon as possible"?

### Effort

-   Have you taken the heavy work off the client, or do you leave it all to them?
-   Can you list in one line the single thing they have to do?
-   How many decisions do you ask them to make before starting? Can you cut them down to one?
-   Have you checked every "all you need to do is" in your text?
-   Do you hand over the finished thing, or only explanations of how to do it?

### Risk and price

-   What happens if it doesn't work? If there is no answer, the price will always seem high.
-   Is your guarantee tied to something verifiable, not to "if you're not satisfied"?
-   Could you honor it tomorrow without putting your business at risk?
-   Do you know how to answer "that's expensive" without offering a discount?
-   Is the price justified by what you take off the client, not by the number of hours you spend?

### Clarity

-   Could you explain your offer in one sentence to a random passerby?
-   Can a person outside your field retell it to someone else without distortion?
-   Have you read it out loud without stumbling on a single phrase?
-   Have you already tested it in five real conversations?
-   Do people ask your price before you name it yourself?

If any answer makes you uncomfortable, that is the weak spot. It is exactly what you should add to the prompt above so the AI works through it with you.

## Takeaways

The most important points, without embellishment:

-   **Value is a fraction.** On top: how desirable the result is, multiplied by how believable it is. On the bottom: how long you have to wait, multiplied by how much work you have to do.
-   **The biggest gains are at the bottom.** Removing the delay and the effort is more believable, cheaper, and harder to copy than promising more.
-   **The result is phrased in the client's words** and must be visible as a scene, not understood as a concept.
-   **Without a timeframe there is no offer.** Two dates: the date of the first visible effect and the date of the full result.
-   **The guarantee is tied to something verifiable.** A date, a deliverable, a number. Never satisfaction.
-   **A discount does not fix a weak offer.** Spend the same money on taking work off the client.
-   **Four steps:** break down what you have, strengthen the four elements in order, rewrite, test on real people.
-   **The prompts evaluate and suggest; you decide what you can deliver.** Never publish a promise you won't be able to keep on Monday.
-   **The sign that things are going well** is that people ask how much it costs and when they can start before you bring it up yourself.

Start today with the smallest thing: take your current description, cross out all the adjectives, and write one single sentence under it with the result and the timeframe. That alone will get half the work done.
