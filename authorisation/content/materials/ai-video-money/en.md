## 🎬 What is Veo 3?

 **Veo 3** is the newest AI-based video generation model from Google, which lets users create high-quality videos from simple text or an image. It's known for its ability to create 1080p video with realistic physics and native sound, including synchronized dialogue, sound effects, and background music. It's designed to give creators a high degree of control, allowing them to craft compelling narratives and cinematic scenes without needing extensive video production resources. In this express course I'll start with the interface and capabilities of VEO specifically, and then smoothly move on to aggregator platforms for video models, where you can choose other models for video generation, such as Kling, Sora, Seedance, and so on. Everything described in this express course applies to absolutely any model, and you can use this knowledge and these principles when working with any neural network that generates video.

## Solving common problems

 Based on the current state of the model, here are some of the most common problems Veo 3 users encounter, along with suggested solutions from the user community.

### **Character drift in long scenes**

> **Problem:** in a long sequence in Scene Builder (usually 5+ frames), a character's facial features or clothing may start to change slightly, even when using a visual reference image. **Solution:** if rerunning the prompt doesn't help, the best solution is to start a new sequence in Scene Builder. Use the last *correct* frame from the previous scene as the new visual reference image for the first frame of the new sequence. This will "reset" the AI's memory and re-anchor the character's appearance.

### **The "breathing" or "wobbling" effect**

> **Problem:** in frames that should be completely static, background objects or even character outlines may have a subtle, unnatural "wobble" or "breathing" from frame to frame. **Solution:** The most effective workaround is to add an almost imperceptible camera movement to the command. By specifying a command like `"a very slow, barely noticeable push-in"` or `"an extremely slow pan to the right,"` the intentional camera movement overrides the unwanted wobble generated by the AI, resulting in a much cleaner frame.

### **Audio dialogue "stutter"**

> **Problem:** A character sometimes repeats a word in a line of dialogue (for example, "is is" or "the the"),**which** is an artifact of audio generation. **Solution:** a negative prompt won't solve this **problem**. The quickest solution is to simply repeat the exact same request, since the AI will generate a new audio track that will usually be correct. If the problem persists, it's often enough to slightly rephrase the dialogue (for example, replacing "The deal is off" with "This deal is off") to make the AI generate a clean result.

### **Ignoring framing commands**

> **Problem:** (Shoot in close-up, but not too close) The AI sometimes ignores a specific shot-type command (for example, "medium shot") in favor of what it considers a more "pleasing" composition (for example, a close-up), especially if the prompt contains many keywords describing facial details. **Solution:** overload the prompt with specifics. Combine a positive instruction, a geometric description, and a negative prompt. For example: `static medium shot, framed from the waist to the top of the head. His torso and arms should be visible. --no close-up` 💡 It's important to understand that most problems that arise in Veo 3 are not critical failures, but rather subtle misinterpretations, where the AI, faced with an ambiguous instruction, defaults to a more general or typical result. For this reason, the general solution that most users reach for first is often the simplest: rerunning the same request, since a different generation can often solve the problem. However, this can be a costly fix in terms of credits if done too often.
>

## 1.1 Introduction: the director's chair

A good prompt serves as both a **script** and a **storyboard**. As a script, it contains precise dialogue, narration, and key sound effects you want to hear in the final version of the video. As a storyboard, it uses descriptive words to paint a visual picture for the AI, defining the characters, their appearance, the setting, and the placement of important objects in the frame.

In addition, your prompt acts as a **director's command**, with which you turn a simple idea into a cinematic scene. This is where you make artistic choices about *how* the story will be filmed. You control the position, angle, and movement of the virtual camera, and you dictate the lighting, mood, and overall visual style. This is the difference between simply stating events and creating a complete, compelling vision for the AI to bring to life.

> 💡 Prompts for Veo 3 are fundamentally different from prompts for a standard large language model, because you're not manipulating text — you're directing a virtual film crew.

## 1.2 The core components: "Who, what, where"

> ### **The subject (who/what)**
>
> The subject is the central element of your prompt that tells the AI about the main character or object to create, and sets the core goal of your scene. **Example:**

```
Rick Sanchez from the cartoon "Rick and Morty" maintains a calm yet independent demeanor. Dressed in a crisp dark suit, his professional demeanor contrasts with the massive gold chain around his neck, which features a large, ornate pendant shaped like the letter "AI." His eyes are hidden behind dark aviator sunglasses with shiny lenses.
```

> ### **The action (what they do)**
>
> The action is the engine of your prompt; it's the verb that brings your static subject to life and gives your video a purpose or narrative. Without a clear action, you'll just have a portrait, but with a strong action, you start to tell a story. **Example:**

```
He deftly rereads a thick wad of bills, slowly drawing on a thick cigar. Exhaling clouds of smoke, he then lets out a deep, confident laugh.
```

> ### The setting (where)
>
> **The location** is an important part of your prompt, because it creates context and atmosphere for your action. It tells the AI where the story takes place, which affects everything: from the background image to the lighting and the overall atmosphere of the scene. A well-described setting, such as "a rain-soaked alley at night," makes your video far more immersive and believable than an action happening in a void. **Example:**

```
In a spacious, top-floor penthouse office. Through the floor-to-ceiling glass walls behind him, the sprawling city lights glitter against the warm, humid tropical night. The room itself is furnished with minimalist, expensive taste, the only light coming from a single desk lamp that casts a warm glow on the stacks of cash and the thick cloud of cigar smoke hanging in the air.
```

> ### Style (light, color, photorealism/cartoon, etc.)
>
> What exactly should you specify for a successful generation? To get a 10/10 on the first try, I recommend including the following components in the style block: 1. Medium (the material it's rendered in) You need to clearly tell the AI "what" it's drawn with. Examples: Digital illustration, Oil painting, 3D render (Octane Render/Unreal Engine 5), Vector art, Watercolor. 1. Lighting and atmosphere Light is 70% of the success. It creates volume. Examples: Cinematic lighting, Volumetric fog, Neon glow, Golden hour, Soft studio light. 1. Technical camera parameters (for photorealism) or detail This defines the "quality" of the image. Examples: 8k resolution, Highly detailed, Unreal Engine 5 style, Macro photography, Deep focus. 1. A specific style or artist If you have a brand book or a reference, you need to specify it. Examples: Ghibli style, Cyberpunk aesthetic, Minimalism, Bauhaus, or in our case — Rick and Morty animation style. 1. Color palette Drawing on Josef Albers' theory of color ("Interaction of Color"), we specify colors to create a mood. Examples: Pastel colors, Monochromatic, Vibrant neon, Earthy tones. The most common mistake is writing "beautiful" or "high quality." To the AI, these are empty words. Use technical terms: "Sharp focus," "Subsurface scattering," or "Cell shaded." **Example:**

```
Visual Style: Authentic Rick and Morty-style 2D animation, crisp black outlines (thick outlines), flat colors without soft shadows, simplified character anatomy, distinctive eye design (star-shaped pupils), pastel sci-fi lab background, Adult Swim aesthetic.
```

> ### Putting it all together
>
> Now let's combine all the elements and see what Veo 3 produces. - **Prompt for the show:**


## 1.3 The art of description: adding detail and mood

 Adding detail and mood turns your prompt from a simple command into a director's complete vision. By defining the atmosphere with lighting, weather, and style, you control the mood of the scene and turn an ordinary clip into a captivating cinematic piece.

* * *

> ### Adjectives are power
>
> Adjectives are the main tool for adding detail and specificity to your prompt, giving the AI concrete visual information to build on. They turn a generic object, such as "a robot," into something unique, for example *"a vintage chrome* robot," which leads to a far more textured and interesting final video.
>
> ### Control over the atmosphere
>
> Controlling the atmosphere lets you dictate the overall mood and emotional tone of your video, giving the AI specific commands about the environment. Using keywords such as "dramatic chiaroscuro" for lighting or "gentle rain" for weather creates a powerful context that influences every visual element.
>
> ### Defining the style
>
> Defining the style gives the AI a powerful artistic tool, instructing it to render the entire scene in a specific, recognizable aesthetic. This command goes beyond simple description, affecting everything from the color palette and linework to character design and overall texture. By requesting a style such as "Ghibli anime" or "1940s film noir," you can create highly targeted videos that instantly evoke a particular genre and mood.
>
> Here's a video generated with a very simple prompt:

```
A man swinging a baseball bat.
```

> > 💬 With a very basic prompt, Veo 3 will create a literal and often generic video, resembling AI-generated stock footage. Because the prompt lacks any creative direction, the model defaults to the most standard interpretation: the subject will be generic, the camera angle will be at eye level and static, and the overall style will be photorealistic but without any specific mood or artistic style. It will show you exactly what you asked for, but nothing more. If you want a specific look or style, you need to be more descriptive and use specific keywords to set the lighting, atmosphere, and overall artistic aesthetic.

## 1.4 Controlling the virtual camera

 Now that you know the core components and the techniques for adding extra detail, it's time to learn about another important aspect of Veo 3: camera angles. By mastering these commands, you gain precise control over the viewer's perspective, telling the AI exactly how to shoot the action. This is the key to creating shots with a professional cinematic effect, rather than just generating a basic static video.

> ### Shot types
>
> Shot types are commands that tell the AI how far the camera should be from the subject, defining how much of the subject will be visible in the frame. Their purpose is to guide the viewer's attention and control the emotional context of the scene; a wide shot can establish the location and make the subject of the frame small, while a close-up can create intimacy and emphasize a character's emotion or an important detail.

Below are examples of the 5 most common shot types used in media.

> > **Wide shot:** shows the entire subject from head to toe and its relationship to the surroundings.
> >
> > **Medium shot (MS):** shows the character from the waist up, balancing detail and body language.
> >
> > **Close-up (CU):** fills the screen with the subject's face to emphasize emotion.
> >
> > **Extreme close-up (ECU):** focuses on a single detail to create intensity or emphasize importance.
> >
> > **Over-the-shoulder shot (OTS):** a view from behind one person looking at another, letting viewers feel like part of the conversation. To include shot types in your own prompts, simply place the command at the very beginning of the description, for example "Wide shot..." or "Close-up...". This tells the AI to first establish the camera framing and only then consider the subject or action, which leads to more reliable and predictable results. ☝ **Pro tip:** **Veo 3** can sometimes misinterpret even a clear command. To get exactly the composition you want, be as specific as possible. Don't just name the shot type — describe the final composition (for example, "a shot from the waist up showing the torso and arms") and add negative constraints (for example, `--no close-up`) so the AI doesn't default to the wrong shot. **For a more detailed explanation of all the shot types, see the video below:**
>
> ### Camera angles
>
> **Camera angles** are commands that define the position and orientation of the virtual camera in relation to the subject. Their role in a **Veo 3** prompt is to go beyond simply framing the shot and start manipulating the viewer's psychological perception of the scene. By choosing a specific angle — high, low, side, or even tilted — you can instantly make a character feel powerful, weak, mysterious, or unbalanced, giving you a powerful tool for shaping the emotional narrative of your video.

Here are a few examples of camera angles in action:

> > **Low angle:** The camera is positioned below the subject's eye line, looking up at them. This angle makes the subject look powerful, dominant, and heroic.
> >
> > **High-angle shot:** the camera is positioned above the subject's eye line and looks down at them. This angle can make the subject look vulnerable, small, or insignificant.
> >
> > **Dutch angle (or Dutch tilt):** the camera is tilted to the side, so the horizon isn't level. This creates a feeling of discomfort, tension, or disorientation in the viewer. 💡 You should place the camera angle command at the beginning of your prompt, right after the shot type, to establish the camera's point of view before considering any other elements. ☝ **Pro tip:** to maximize the effect of a camera angle, combine it with a matching action or expression from the character. For example, a low-angle shot will be more striking if the character is also confidently looking down, and a high-angle shot will be more effective if the character is looking up with vulnerability. Don't be afraid to use modifiers such as "subtle" or "extreme" to control the intensity of the angle and achieve exactly the emotional tone you're after. ## 2.2 Camera movement: beyond the static shot

While Chapter 1 was dedicated to static framing, professional shoots include movement to guide the eye and reveal information.

> 💡 **Camera movements** are used to turn the viewer from a passive observer into an active participant, adding dynamism and energy to a scene. They serve an important narrative function — following the action, revealing new information, or directing the viewers' attention to important details. Ultimately, these intentional movements create a more immersive and emotionally engaging experience than a simple static shot.

### Basic movements

> ### **Panning**
>
> This is when the camera rotates horizontally from a left position to a right one, or from right to left, from a fixed position. Its purpose is often to follow a character's movement across the scene or to slowly reveal the expanse of a location.
>

>
> ### Tilt
>
> This is when the camera rotates vertically, up or down, from a fixed position. Its purpose is usually to show the height of an object or a character (for example, tilting up a tall building) or to follow a vertical action, such as a falling object.
>

>
> ### Dolly Zoom
>
> This is when the entire camera physically moves forward toward the subject ("dolly in") or backward away from it ("dolly out"). A dolly in is used to create intimacy and focus on a character's emotional reaction, while a dolly out can reveal a wider context or create a sense of detachment.
>
> ### Tracking
>
> This is when the entire camera physically moves sideways, parallel to the subject. Its purpose is to follow the subject as it moves, keeping the same distance from it and holding it in the frame.

### Advanced cinematic techniques

> ### Dolly zoom, or the "vertigo" shot
>
> This is a powerful effect achieved by physically moving the camera toward or away from the subject while simultaneously zooming the lens in the opposite direction. As a result, the subject stays the same size in the frame, but the background appears to stretch or compress dramatically, creating a disorienting visual distortion. This technique is used to visualize a moment of intense psychological distress in a character, a sudden realization, or the feeling that their world has become unstable.
>
> ### Rack focus
>
> This is a filming technique in which the lens focus is intentionally shifted from one subject to another within a single shot, without any cuts. It creates an effect in which one subject goes from sharp focus to blurry, while a second subject, located on a different depth plane, simultaneously goes from blurry to sharp. This is a powerful directorial technique used to draw the viewers' attention, reveal new important information, or create a direct narrative connection between two characters or objects in a scene. 💡 Although this guide covers the basic camera movements, the language of cinema is vast and full of creative techniques. Movements such as crane shots soaring into the air, sharp whip pans that create energy, and complex tracking shots traveling through an entire scene are all part of a director's arsenal.
>
> I encourage you to explore these advanced techniques on your own and experiment with them in your assignments, to give your Veo 3 videos even more dynamism and a professional style. **Additional learning:**

## Adding camera movement to Veo 3 prompts

To add camera movement to a prompt, you should describe the camera's action as if it were the main subject, using clear, directional verbs. The best place for this command is at the very beginning of your prompt, right after the shot type, so that the AI establishes the movement before rendering the content of the scene.

**Example prompt:**

```
The camera performs a slow pan from right to left, revealing a single coffee cup sitting on a wooden table.
```

**Result:**

> 💡 **Pro tip:** for the most effective results, link the camera movement directly to the subject's action (for example, "The camera performs a fast sideways move, following a running person"). Also use descriptive adverbs such as "slow," "smooth," or "jerky" to control the speed and feel of the movement, which gives you more control over the directing.

## 2.3 The director's veto: negative prompts

### What are negative prompts?

Negative prompts serve as a "director's veto," giving you an important ability to explicitly forbid the AI from including certain elements or actions in your video. This is an essential tool for refining the result and preventing common mistakes, such as automatically generated subtitles with dialogue or unwanted camera movements. By telling the AI what *not* to do, you gain a more precise level of control in cases where a positive instruction alone is not enough to achieve the desired result. The most common negative prompts are used to solve frequent problems and reinforce certain creative decisions. They are usually added at the end of the prompt using the `--no` command. **Below are some of the most useful examples:** | Negative prompt | Purpose | |---|---| | --no subtitles, text, captions | Keeps the video frame clean by removing any on-screen text | | --no walking | Keeps the character still when only the camera should move | | --no camera panning, tilting, or zooming | Ensures a completely static shot without any camera movement | **Other useful examples:** | Negative prompt | Purpose | |---|---| | --no flickering | Reduces unwanted flickering of light or the frame in the video | | --no logo | Prevents the appearance of automatically generated or placeholder logos | | --no watermark | Removes any system-added watermarks for a cleaner output | | --no blur | Prevents automatic motion blur or background blur | | --no background change | Keeps the background unchanged across all frames to avoid abrupt transitions |

> 💡 **Pro tip:** use negative prompts like a scalpel, not a hammer. Your first instinct should always be to use strong, positive descriptions to guide the AI in the direction you want. Avoid using negative prompts for creative direction — for example, instead of asking to create a `character --no smiling,` it's much more effective to ask to create a `character with a serious, stoic expression`.
>
> Save negative prompts for troubleshooting, such as removing persistent technical errors like subtitles (`--no subtitles`), or eliminating a specific unwanted element that the AI keeps generating despite your positive instructions.

### **Negative prompts in action:**

## 3.1 Introduction: beyond silent film

Built-in audio support **in Veo 3** radically changes the rules of the game in the field of AI-assisted video creation. Now, instead of silent clips, you can create full audiovisual scenes with dialogue and sound effects in a single step. This opens up possibilities for creating more immersive, story-driven videos with talking characters and rich sound — all with a single command. In this chapter, you'll learn how to master sound design and dialogue for video creation, giving you the tools to build a complete and engaging audiovisual experience.

* * *

> Before Veo 3, adding sound to an AI-generated video was a complex and manual process. Users first had to generate the video without sound and then separately create or find all the audio elements — dialogue with text-to-speech tools, sound effects from online libraries, and music with AI or stock sources. The biggest challenge was synchronizing the dialogue with the characters' mouth movements, which was rarely accurate. After gathering all the audio resources, users had to import everything into a video editor such as Premiere Pro or DaVinci Resolve. There they manually layered and aligned each audio track with the visual material — a labor-intensive and often imperfect process that required significant effort and technical skill.

## 3.2 Prompts for dialogue

### The basic command

The simplest way to make a character speak in Veo 3 is to use a simple, direct command structure. After describing the subject, you use a verb such as `"says"` `, "shouts," or` `"whispers,"` followed by the exact dialogue you want to hear, enclosed in standard quotation marks. The AI uses the text inside these quotes to generate a synthesized voice that speaks the phrase, and it automatically tries to sync the character's mouth movements with the spoken words.

**Example prompt:**

```
{
 "prompt_metadata": {
 "prompt_name": "Universal_Pixel_Existentialism",
 "base_style": "High-quality cinematic style, matching the visual texture and lighting of the source image.",
 "visual_elements": {
 "subject": "The primary character from the uploaded image, maintaining exact facial features, clothing, and proportions.",
 "environment": "The background environment from the source image, with enhanced atmospheric depth and realistic lighting.",
 "lighting": "Cinematic lighting that highlights the subject's expressions, matching the original source light direction."
 },
 "action_and_movement": {
 "camera": "Completely static Medium Shot. No zoom, no pan, no camera shake.",
 "primary_action": "The character looks directly into the camera lens with a look of sudden, haunting realization. Their lips move in perfect sync with the dialogue.",
```

### Directing the performance

Directing the performance is crucial, because the same phrase can have a completely different meaning depending on *how* it is spoken. Simply telling the AI `"A man says: 'I know what you did'"` will produce a neutral, generic intonation `.`

By adding a simple adverb, you give the AI a direct emotional instruction, much like a director gives direction to an actor. This lets you control the subtext and tone of the scene, ensuring the character's performance matches your narrative goals.

**Below are examples with three different dialogue directions:**

**In a whisper**

```
A medium close-up shot of an attractive woman with soft features and gentle eyes. She sits in a dimly lit, cozy room with warm lighting and a softly glowing background. She leans slightly toward the camera and whispers in an ASMR-style voice, “You are safe here… just relax and let the stress melt away.” Her tone is slow, soothing, and delicate, with a calming expression that matches the softness of her voice.
```

* * *

**Intense scream**

```
A handheld, shaky close-up of a terrified woman trapped in a dark, blood-splattered hallway. Her makeup is smeared and her hands tremble as she slams her fists against a locked wooden door. With tears streaming down her face, she throws her head back and screams at the top of her lungs, “LET ME OUT! PLEASE, SOMEBODY HELP ME!”
```

* * *

**Nervous speech**

```
A medium shot of a cartoon-style young man with oversized glasses, messy hair, and a sweater vest. He stands in front of a group of other characters in a bright, exaggerated school hallway. He fidgets with his hands, eyes darting nervously. His voice quivers and stutters as he says, “I-I-I didn't mean to ruin everything… I swear! It was an accident!”
```

> Keep in mind that the AI's voice may not always exactly match your intended tone. If that happens, simply try running the prompt again or adjust the prompt for a better result.

### Creating dialogue between multiple characters

You can create a dialogue by writing the script line by line, clearly attributing each line to a specific character. This helps the AI understand the flow of the conversation and assign each speaker the correct voice and tone. Be sure to clearly format the dialogue, using each character's name followed by their line, so the conversation sounds natural and is easy to follow. **Example prompt:**

```
A medium shot of two characters, a man and a woman, sitting across from each other at a small café table on a quiet street corner. The scene is softly lit by the warm afternoon sun filtering through a striped canopy. The man, dressed in a crisp button-up shirt, leans forward slightly, his voice low and conflicted as he says, “We can't keep meeting like this..”

The woman, wearing a light summer dress, looks away briefly, exhaling a deep sigh before meeting his gaze. Her voice is tinged with both sadness and resolve as she replies, “I understand, but what other choice do we have?”
```

> 💡 **Problems and considerations:** Despite its effectiveness, this technique can be tricky. The AI may sometimes assign a character the wrong voice or struggle with the framing to keep both speakers in the shot. It's very important to have clear character descriptions and to be ready to rerun the prompt to get the perfect result. ☝ **Pro tips:** - **Use clear descriptions:** when you describe characters for the first time, make their features as distinct as possible (for example, "a tall man with short black hair" versus "a short woman with long blonde hair"). This helps the AI create and track two clearly distinguished visual identities. - **Limit the dialogue to one line per character:** avoid writing long dialogues in a single prompt. It's more reliable when one character delivers their line and then, using the Scene Builder, a new shot is created for the other character's reply. - **Control the camera:** if the AI struggles to frame both speakers correctly, give it clear **directions**. Use prompts `such as "A medium shot of a man and a woman"` or `"Shot over the shoulder, looking at the man,"` to force the AI to create the composition you want `.` - **Rerun the prompt:** sometimes the AI simply assigns a character the wrong voice. Often the fastest fix is to simply rerun the same prompt to get a new generation with the correct voice assignments.

## 3.3 Sound effects and atmosphere

### Sound effects

Prompting for specific sound effects (SFX) means you're acting as a virtual sound-effects artist, telling the AI exactly which sound to generate for a specific on-screen action. SFX are distinct, instantaneous sounds that should be directly tied to a visual event to make the scene more immersive and believable. To do this effectively, you should describe the action and then, in the same sentence, describe the sound it produces. This creates a strong contextual link that helps the AI correctly synchronize the audio and video.

> 💡 Veo 3 often generates sound effects automatically, because its main goal is to create a realistic scene, and most real-world actions are accompanied by a corresponding sound. However, it's still very important to specify particular sound effects, because this gives you directorial control over the *character* and *quality* of the sound. **Example prompt 1 (action-based SFX):**

```
A close-up of a heavy, antique book being dropped onto a dusty wooden table. The book lands with a loud, satisfying **thud**, sending a small cloud of dust into the air.
```

> **Example prompt 2 (environment-based SFX):**

```
A wide shot of a dark, stormy sky. A brilliant fork of lightning splits the clouds, followed immediately by the deep, echoing **crack of thunder**.
```

* * *

### Atmosphere

Atmosphere is the background soundscape of a scene, which defines the character and reality of a place. It's not a specific, instantaneous sound effect, but a continuous, background set of noises that tells the viewers where and when the action takes place — for example, the quiet chirping of crickets at night or the low hum of city traffic. To add atmosphere to a Veo 3 prompt, you should describe the background sounds as part of the description of the setting. This is different from prompting for a specific sound effect; you are telling the AI what the environment itself sounds like. **Example prompt:**

```
{
 "prompt": {
 "visual": "8K cinematic photorealistic video. A gaunt 70-year-old man with messy grey hair and deep wrinkles, a scar over his left eyebrow. He wears a soiled white laboratory coat over a black sweater, dark work pants, and scuffed brown boots. He sits cross-legged on fine beach sand, hunched forward, staring at the distant horizon. Background: a deserted Caribbean beach at sunset, orange sky, soft waves. Beside him is a weathered wooden porch with salt-corroded timber. NO sparks, NO glowing particles, NO digital effects. Static camera with a slow, smooth Dolly-In to his face.",
 "audio": "High-fidelity binaural soundscape: rhythmic heavy waves crashing on sand, clear piercing cries of seagulls from the distance, soft wind whistling. NO MUSIC. Audio matches the melancholic lounge pace.",
 "negative_prompt": "sparks, glowing particles, digital ash, magic energy, pixelated data, fire, light flares, fantasy effects, text, subtitles, watermark, logo, shaky camera, blurry textures, modern buildings, branding, young face, happy expression.",
 "settings": {
 "aspect_ratio": "16:9",
 "duration": "8s",
 "resolution": "8k"
 }
 }
}
```

> ☝ **Pro tips:** - **Create depth by layering sounds.** A truly immersive environment has several layers of sound. Instead of just requesting a single sound, create a sense of depth by describing both the main close sound and a secondary distant one. For example: **"The main sound is the quiet lapping of waves on the shore, with a faint, distant sound of bass music coming from a resort down the beach."** - **Use ambient sounds to contrast with the action.** Sound can be a powerful storytelling tool when it contrasts with the action on screen. You can create a more poignant or tense scene by contrasting a quiet, emotional moment with a loud, indifferent background. For example: **"A close-up of a man's face, with a single tear rolling down his cheek. In the background, the loud, cheerful ambient sounds of a street festival in Santo Domingo continue, full of laughter and music."**

## 3.4 Creating mood with music

Adding a musical accompaniment is one of the most effective ways to set the emotional tone of your video. While you can't request a specific copyrighted song or artist, you can guide the Veo 3 music generator by describing the genre, mood, and even the instrumentation you want to hear.

To do this, add a sentence to your prompt describing the music as another element of the scene, which lets you create custom music that perfectly matches the mood of your visuals.

* * *

**Example prompt 1: EDM**

```
A wide shot of a fit woman in athletic swimwear sprinting down a dark, rain-slicked alley at night. Her long hair trails behind her as she runs, muscles tense with determination. **A tense, driving electronic score with a heavy, pulsing synth bass builds in the background, increasing the sense of urgency.**
```

> **Example prompt 2: jazz music**

```
Medium shot: A sophisticated fat cat lounges in a plush, comfortable armchair by a warm crackling fireplace, casually smoking a cigarette. > ☝ **Pro tips:** - **Be specific about instruments.** Don't just ask for a genre; guide the AI by mentioning specific instruments. Instead of "sad music," try **"sad music with a lonely piano."** This gives the model a much clearer creative target and results in more specific and expressive music than something generic. - **Direct the action of the music.** Treat the music as a character in your scene, using verbs. A prompt can be much more effective if the music isn't static. For example, **"Tense orchestral music builds in the background"** is more effective than simply "tense orchestral music plays," because it adds dynamics and a narrative arc to the audio.
```

## 3.5 Troubleshooting common audio problems

> ### **Audio limitations and problems in Veo 3**
>
> Although Veo 3 offers impressive audio integration, problems can still arise — for example, the audio may not play at all. Knowing about these limitations in advance will help you adjust your prompts and work more efficiently. - **Dialogue glitches:** the AI may sometimes stutter, repeat words, or mispronounce unusual names and slang expressions. - **Tone mismatch:** the emotional tone of the dialogue may sometimes not match the adverb in the prompt (for example, sounding cheerful when the prompt calls for sounding angry). - **Conflicts between actions and dialogue:** audio generation often fails if a character performs an action that physically makes speaking difficult, such as running or being underwater. - **Lack of voice control:** you can't directly choose a specific voice type, accent, or gender; the model selects them based on its interpretation of the prompt. - **Music specificity:** while you can request a genre or mood, you can't request a specific, well-known song or artist. - **Sound-effect volume:** the volume of generated sound effects can sometimes be inconsistent, too loud or too quiet for the scene.
>
> ### **Quick fixes for audio errors**
>
> -   **Rerun the prompt:** often the fastest fix is to simply rerun the very same prompt. The AI may generate the audio correctly on a repeat attempt.
> -   **Simplify the dialogue:** break long, complex sentences into shorter, more direct ones.
> -   **Add a directing adverb:** if the tone is wrong, be more specific. Instead of `"He says: ..."` try `"He calmly says: ...,"` to give the AI a clear emotional goal.
> -   **Check the punctuation****:** make sure all the dialogue is enclosed in standard quotation marks (`"..."`) and that there are no typos in it.
> -   **Isolate the audio:** if you suspect a conflict, try a new prompt containing only the character and the dialogue (for example, `"A man says: 'Your phrase here'"`). If it works, the problem was probably a conflict with an action in your original prompt `.` Although generative video has been around for some time, combining visual elements with AI-generated audio — such as dialogue, music, and sound effects in a single prompt — is still a relatively new and rapidly developing feature. Veo 3 is being actively improved, so even if everything isn't working perfectly right now, over time the audio features will become more reliable, expressive, and powerful.

## 4.1 Introduction: the problem of integrity

Creating one beautiful shot is impressive, but for storytelling, your world and its inhabitants need to look the same in every frame. This is the biggest challenge for AI-generated video.

### Why is consistency so important?

Consistency in AI-generated videos is crucial, because it helps reduce the sense of artificiality, or the "AI effect," that often breaks immersion in the story. When characters suddenly change their appearance, voice, or mannerisms between shots — or when the visual style changes unexpectedly — it reminds viewers that they're watching something artificial.

Maintaining consistency in character design, behavior, and tone helps create the illusion of continuity and intentionality, making the content feel more like a cohesive work than a collection of disjointed fragments. This is especially important when creating emotionally engaging or professional-looking videos that hold the viewer's attention.

This chapter presents the main strategies and techniques for maintaining a unified visual style for both the characters and the overall artistic style across all clips.

> 💡 **Although perfect consistency between shots isn't always possible, I can show you techniques that will help maintain a strong sense of continuity between your characters and environments.**

## 4.3 Maintaining a Single Aesthetic Style

Just as consistency in a character's appearance matters, maintaining a single aesthetic or style across all your video clips is essential for keeping viewers immersed and the narrative continuous. In Veo 3, the output is photorealistic by default, unless your request explicitly indicates a different look or implies a clear setting. This means that, unless you introduce stylized elements such as cel-shading visual effects, painterly textures, or cinematic color grading, your scenes will naturally tend toward realism.

### Creating a "Style Prompt"

While **character consistency** is about ensuring a specific person or object looks identical from one frame to the next, **style consistency** is about ensuring the overall artistic *feel* of the video stays unchanged. The latter, though it needs attention, is more forgiving. If you consistently use "style prompting" such as `"cinematic, film noir, high-contrast lighting"`, the AI can reliably apply that aesthetic. Even if there are small differences in shadows or colors between frames, the overall mood stays the same.

### How to Create a Style Prompt

> -   **Start with the main style:** begin with a single high-level keyword that defines the overall aesthetic.
> -   *Example:* `comic book style`
> -   **Add lighting-specific keywords:** reinforce the style by adding specific lighting cues that match the mood.
> -   *Example:* `comic book style, bold shadows, dramatic comic-book lighting`
> -   **Add color and texture:** refine the look with keywords for the color palette and visual texture.
> -   *Example:* `comic book style, sharp shadows, dramatic comic-book lighting, vivid colors, halftone texture`
> -   **Refine the style prompt:** if the video didn't turn out the way you imagined, you'll need to rework the prompt to get the aesthetic you want.
> -   *Example:* `highly stylized comic-book illustration, vivid inks, thick black outlines, exaggerated shadows, bright flat colors, halftone texture, 2D graphic novel, zero photorealism.`
> -   **Save your "style bible":** this final string of keywords is your project's "style bible". Save it in your notes so you can easily copy and paste it into every prompt for this project, ensuring a perfectly unified aesthetic look from frame to frame. ## 5.1 Introduction: from director to architect

> There's a simple way to make your prompts dramatically more effective and instantly clear to the AI. It's like having a superpower for crafting prompts. It's called JavaScript Object Notation, or JSON for short.

* * *

**JSON** is a simple way to store and exchange data that is understandable to both humans and computers. You can think of it as a neatly organized shopping list written in a very specific format — it has **labels** (keys) and **values** (the actual information), and it's always organized so that computers can read it easily. For example:

```
{
 "name": "Alex",
 "age": 25,
 "isStudent": true
}
```

In the example above, we see: - `"name"` is the label, and `"Alex"` is the value. - `"age"` is the label, and `25` is the value. - `"isStudent"` is the label, and `true` means "yes". It's used almost everywhere — on websites, in apps, and in APIs — to send and receive information in a clean, predictable format. If natural language is like talking to a director, then JSON is like handing him a detailed architectural blueprint. In image and video prompts this structure is effective because it organizes every element of your request into clear, labeled sections that the AI can interpret without guessing. The reusability of JSON makes it a powerful tool for generating images and video, because it lets you save perfectly structured requests that you can use again and again without losing clarity or detail. Once you've defined elements like style, theme, settings, and camera movement, you can quickly adjust only what's necessary while keeping the rest unchanged, which guarantees stable results across repeated uses. This not only speeds up the creative process, but also makes it easier to maintain continuity in multi-scene projects, giving you precise control over the final result every time.

> **In this chapter you'll learn about JSON prompting and several simple ways to use this technique without having to format anything by hand.**

## 5.2 The Anatomy of a JSON Prompt

### **Key-Value Pairs**

At its core, JSON works with **pairs** — a *key* (the label) and a *value* (the information). The key tells the AI what type of information it's looking at, and the value provides the actual details. This clear structure ensures the AI knows exactly how to interpret each part of your prompt without confusion.

### **Core Objects**

These are the main building blocks in Veo 3 JSON prompts. Each object plays a specific role in shaping the generated image or video:

-   `**description**` — the main, vivid narrative of the scene. Here you describe in detail the explosive transformation, the assembly of elements, and the overall cinematic moment.
-   `**style**` — describes the visual tone and rendering style (e.g., "cinematic, photorealistic", "realistic sci-fi action"). This tells Veo 3 what look and feel to aim for.
-   `**camera**` — defines camera movements, angles, and transitions (e.g., dolly, crane, orbital shots). Adds dynamic movement to the scene.
-   `**lighting**` — defines the lighting setup and any changes over the sequence (e.g., golden-hour glow, neon city lights, dramatic spotlights).
-   `**environment**` — the physical setting or background (e.g., "futuristic industrial corridor", "sun-drenched café").
-   `**elements**` — a list of all the visual components of the scene as a bulleted list — props, characters, effects, environmental details — that Veo 3 should render.
-   `**motion**` — the choreography of the action — how elements move, assemble, transform, or interact dynamically over the sequence.
-   `**ending**` — the final composed scene, describing how the shot ends.
-   `**text**` — any text overlaid on the video (e.g., taglines, or none). In most cinematic scenes this parameter is set to `"none"`.
-   `**keywords**` — a list of relevant tags to sharpen the prompt's targeting. Often includes keywords related to aspect ratio, style, subject matter, and special effects.

    > 💡 There are other JSON prompt templates with different key pairs, but I recommend this exact one to achieve maximum clarity, flexibility, and consistency in complex image- and video-generation projects.

## Should You Always Use JSON Prompts?

JSON is ideal for video generation when you need precise control over several aspects of a scene, such as description, style, camera movement, lighting, environment, and key elements. Its structured format lets creators clearly define each component, making it easier for the AI to interpret complex instructions without ambiguity. Natural language is preferable when you want to quickly convey an idea, set a tone, or experiment with creative concepts without spending time structuring every detail. It's especially useful for brainstorming, rapid iteration, or when working with simpler scenes that don't require a detailed breakdown of parameters. Knowing both JSON and natural-language prompting gives you flexibility. ### Limitations and Criticism

While JSON prompts are a powerful tool, critics note that for creative tasks such as writing stories or generating nuanced content, the rigid structure of JSON can limit the model's performance, because it switches the AI into a technical mode where format takes priority over creativity. Additionally, JSON can lead to token inefficiency due to its syntax (e.g., brackets and quotation marks), potentially increasing costs in token-based systems. Alternatives such as Markdown or XML can sometimes offer a similar structure with less overhead.
