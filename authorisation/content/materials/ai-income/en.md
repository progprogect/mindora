## 1. How it works

AI is not a business model by itself. Clients pay for a result: saved time, clearer decisions, more consistent content, fewer manual errors or faster service. Start with a real problem, define an observable result and use AI only where it improves the workflow.

This guide does not promise income. Results depend on your skills, the market, positioning, sales effort, quality, costs and local rules. Do not invent case studies, income screenshots or client testimonials. Track gross revenue, tool costs, contractor payments, refunds, taxes and client acquisition costs separately.

## 2. Analyze your useful skills

Make a list of tasks you can already do without AI: writing, design, spreadsheets, research, video editing, customer support, teaching, sales or operations. Then list the parts that AI can speed up. The overlap is your safest first offer, because you can judge the quality instead of blindly forwarding model output.

Choose one audience you can reach and one recurring problem you understand. Before building a package, interview five potential users. Ask what they do now, how often, what it costs, what goes wrong and what a useful result would look like. In the first conversation, focus on the person's task; do not turn the interview into a pushy sales pitch.

## 3. Ten AI service directions

The following models are a starting point, not guaranteed opportunities: research reviews with cited sources; meeting and interview summaries; product description systems; multilingual content adaptation with review by a native speaker; turning long recordings into short videos; spreadsheet cleanup and reporting; internal knowledge base assistants; customer support systems; documentation and workflow automation; and AI literacy training for a specific team.

For each model, sell a defined result, not "AI consulting". Specify the input data, the number of revision rounds, the turnaround time, the exclusions and the human review that is included. Do not take on regulated medical, legal or financial work unless you have the necessary qualifications and oversight.

## How to complete your first small order

Below are ten examples of a limited-scope project. Agree on the input data, the result and the review before you start work. The examples do not mean you already have the necessary skills: choose a task whose quality you can evaluate yourself.

### 1. Overview of information on a given topic

Input: the client's question, the topic boundaries and permitted sources. Collect dated materials, ask AI to pull out the facts relevant to the question, then open every link and verify the conclusions. Output: a short overview with sources and a list of what is unknown. Acceptance: every external claim is backed by a cited source; assumptions are separated from facts.

### 2. Meeting summaries

Input: a recording or transcript that the client has permitted you to process. Correct names and terms, ask AI to pull out decisions and action items, and check disputed phrases against the recording. Output: meeting minutes and a "task — owner — deadline" table. Acceptance: owners and deadlines are not invented; missing information is marked as needing clarification.

### 3. Product descriptions

Input: product cards with verified specifications and formatting requirements. Make one sample, agree on the tone and structure, then process a small batch of products. Output: a table of titles and descriptions. Acceptance: sizes, composition, compatibility and package contents match the source data; missing properties are not invented.

### 4. Adapting materials into another language

Input: the source text, the audience's country, the brand glossary and the format. Prepare the translation, check numbers, links and meaning, and pass the text to a native speaker for editing. Output: an agreed local version and a list of changed examples. Acceptance: the terms of the offer are preserved, and dates and units are clear to the audience. If you cannot arrange a language review, do not promise a publish-ready result.

### 5. Short clips from a long video

Input: a permitted recording and the purpose of publication. Find complete thoughts using the transcript, check the beginning and end against the video, edit the clips and fix the subtitles. Output: an agreed number of short videos. Acceptance: the meaning of the speech is preserved, sentences are not cut off, and the music and images are cleared for use.

### 6. Spreadsheet cleanup and report

Input: a copy of the spreadsheet and rules for handling gaps, dates and duplicates. First identify the column types, propose transformations and agree on the disputed rows. Output: a cleaned copy, a report and a change log. Acceptance: row counts and checksums are reconciled; the original is preserved; disputed records are not silently deleted.

### 7. Internal instructions assistant

Input: an agreed set of current documents. Remove outdated versions, set up answers with links to the source, and prepare questions with known answers and questions outside the knowledge base. Output: a limited prototype and instructions for the owner. Acceptance: the assistant cites the right documents and says when there is not enough data; access rights are checked separately.

### 8. Support reply drafts

Input: anonymized tickets and the company's current rules. Sort the tickets by topic, write templates and set the conditions for handing off to a staff member. Output: a library of replies for an agent to review. Acceptance: no invented refunds, deadlines or discounts; non-standard cases are passed to a human. Do not enable automatic sending in the first project without separate agreement.

### 9. Instructions for a recurring process

Input: the employee's description of the process, examples and constraints. Break the work into steps, specify the inputs, output and exceptions; ask another employee to walk through the instructions on a test example. Output: working instructions and a list of possible improvements. Acceptance: the steps are reproducible, the owners are known, and errors have a clear handling path. Integrations and automation are estimated as a separate scope of work.

### 10. Hands-on session for a team

Input: participants' roles and two or three real tasks that may be used for demonstration. Prepare exercises, example requests and criteria for checking a response. Output: a session, a handout and self-study assignments. Acceptance: participants can repeat the exercise and explain what needs to be checked in an AI response; productivity gains are not promised without measurement.

## 4. Score and choose one offer

Score each idea from one to five for how often the problem occurs, access to buyers, ability to verify quality, turnaround time, likelihood of repeat orders and risk. Score tool costs and requirement complexity in reverse: the lower they are, the higher the score. Choose one idea with a clear buyer and a final result you can demonstrate using synthetic or permitted sample data.

Formulate the offer in one sentence: "I help [specific client] turn [input/problem] into [verifiable result] within [timeframe], including [quality control], without [important risk]." Remove vague words such as "revolutionary", "easy", "guaranteed".

- Success metric: [one measurable criterion]
- Wrap-up: [file handover, export and data deletion]

## 5. Create a reference sample

Create one "before and after" example using your own data or a public source with a clear license. Show the input, the workflow, the human decisions and the final result. Label simulated data. A good sample shows the quality of your judgment, not just your ability to click "Generate".

Record the time spent on each step. Include research, corrections, formatting, communication and delivery. If building the prototype takes six hours, do not estimate it as a one-hour task. Save your prompts and checklists, but keep confidential client data out of public templates.

## 6. Price from economics, not hype

Calculate the minimum price: the estimated working time multiplied by your sustainable hourly minimum, plus tool usage, contractor costs, fees, a rework allowance, taxes and a risk buffer. Compare it with the value and the alternatives available to the buyer. Price the agreed result and scope of work, not the number of prompts.

Before agreeing on a recurring engagement, offer a small paid trial project. Avoid free custom work disguised as a test. A pilot should have a narrow scope, real approval criteria and a decision date. Review profitability after each completed order, using actual hours and costs.

## 7. Find your first conversations

Start with warm contacts, professional communities, former colleagues and businesses whose public materials show a suitable task. Send a short message: one specific observation, one relevant example, one proposed pilot and a low-pressure question. Do not mass-send synthetic personalization and do not collect personal data.

Track the organization you contacted, the lawful source, the date, the response and the next action. Respect opt-out requests and platform rules. The goal of the first outreach is to agree on a conversation and understand the client's task.

## 8. Find out the client's task and agree on the scope

Confirm the current workflow, users, data confidentiality, required integrations, the person responsible for acceptance, success metrics and the consequences of failure. Ask what must never be sent to an external model. Separate the required result from optional automation that can be agreed on later.

Document assumptions and exclusions. If access, data quality or stakeholder approval is missing, postpone agreeing on the project until those conditions are clarified. Never promise fully autonomous operation if the process still requires judgment or when errors materially affect people.

## 9. Check and hand over the result to the client

Use a repeatable chain: receive the agreed input data; remove unnecessary personal and confidential information; perform the described steps; check facts, calculations, links and files; compare the result with the acceptance criteria; get approval from the responsible person; hand over the materials and either keep or delete the data as agreed.

Keep a defect log and a version number. For research, attach the sources. For automation, include failure handling and a way to return to manual work. For content, check claims, rights to materials and required disclosures. Never hide that AI made a substantial contribution when disclosure is required or relevant to trust.

## 10. Turn a pilot project into recurring work

A recurring engagement makes sense if the task comes up repeatedly. Propose a frequency, scope, target service level, a way to review results and a process for making changes. Show what improved during the pilot using measured data: hours saved, turnaround time, error rate or volume of tasks processed.

Do not lock the client into your own workflow without an export. Provide documentation, ownership terms and an exit process. Recurring revenue should come from repeated value, not from making cancellation difficult.

## 11. Keep a simple working dashboard

Every week, record prospects, qualified conversations, proposals, paid pilots, payments received, refunds, tool costs, contractor costs, hours worked and the difference between revenue and direct costs. Separate invoiced amounts from money actually received. Do not count trials, unpaid invoices or renewals that have not happened.

Review conversion by source and by offer. Stop channels that consume time without qualified conversations. Fix bottlenecks using evidence: message, sample, scope, price, delivery quality or retention. One clean dashboard is more useful than optimistic screenshots.

## 12. 14-day validation plan

- Days 1–2: choose one audience and an interview script.
- Days 3–5: complete five conversations.
- Day 6: choose one offer and define the exclusions.
- Days 7–8: create a permitted sample and measure the turnaround time.
- Day 9: set the minimum price and the pilot scope.
- Days 10–12: manually contact ten suitable prospects.
- Days 13–14: review the responses, update the offer and decide whether to continue, change the audience or stop.

The success of this validation is not a promised income figure. It is evidence: a recurring problem, accessible buyers, a result you can verify, acceptable costs in time and money, and at least one substantive discussion of a trial order.

## 13. Seven days to prepare a pilot

The plan helps you prepare and propose a small service. It does not guarantee that demand or sales will appear within a week.

- Day 1: choose one service and an accessible audience. Describe the problem in one sentence.
- Day 2: talk to members of the audience. Write down how the work is done now and what gets in the way.
- Day 3: make a sample using your own or permitted data and measure the total working time.
- Day 4: refine the sample, define the scope, the number of revisions and the pilot price.
- Day 5: prepare a short message and send it personally to suitable contacts.
- Day 6: answer questions, clarify the scope and write down the reasons for any lack of interest.
- Day 7: evaluate the responses, time and expenses. If an agreement is reached, confirm the pilot. Otherwise, reconsider the audience, the problem or the sample.

### First message

Hello, [name]. I noticed [real, specific observation]. I prepared a small sample of [result] using [your own or permitted data]. May I show it to you? If the task is relevant, I propose a pilot: [scope], [deadline] and [number of revisions].

### One-page proposal

- Goal: [verifiable result]
- You provide: [agreed data]
- I deliver: [files and formats]
- Deadline: [date after receiving the complete set of data]
- Revisions: [number and limits]
- Price and payment: [amount, currency, terms]
- Not included: [exclusions]
- Acceptance: [criteria]
- Next step: [required approval]

### Comparing directions and calculating costs

If the work took five hours, the simplified calculation gives 20 per hour. This is not a benchmark of market income. In full accounting, include finding clients, revisions and time without orders.

## 14. Four exercises to prepare your offer

### 1. Assemble a single-service package

- Choose one type of buyer and an observable recurring problem.
- Define the permitted input data and the exact result.
- Describe a paid pilot: revision limits, the person responsible for approval and the exclusions.
- Compare it with the alternatives by verified properties, without invented testimonials.

Help me assemble a package for [service] for [buyer]. First ask about the missing facts. Then prepare a one-page description: input data, deliverables, deadline after receiving the data, the person responsible for approval, revisions, exclusions and success criteria. Mark unverified benefits as hypotheses.

Check: the buyer understands what they will get and what they are responsible for.

### 2. Hold a conversation with a potential buyer

- Arrange a real conversation with the other person's consent.
- Ask about the current process, how often the task occurs, the consequences of delays and previous attempts to solve it.
- Before describing the pilot, restate the problem in your own words and check your understanding.
- Treat objections as information.

Play a skeptical buyer of [service] based on a fictional profile: [profile]. Answer only in their voice. After the exercise, separately list the points on my side that were unclear and the claims that lacked evidence.

Check: in your notes, the interviewee's actual words are kept separate from your interpretation.

### 3. Prepare an offer page

- First agree on the offer and the visitor's main action.
- In the headline, name the specific result, then explain the process and the limitations.
- Add only genuine proof, permitted samples and exact terms.
- Before publishing, check the form, the price, how the result is delivered and the contact links.

Suggest a page structure for [agreed offer], using only [facts]. Include a headline, the audience, the result, the process, exclusions, available proof, answers to questions and one call to action. Move unconfirmed claims into a separate list for review. Do not invent testimonials or scarcity.

Check: every public claim is substantiated, and the button leads to the right next step.

### 4. Rehearse the offer discussion

- Explain the pilot in simple words and ask for clarifications or corrections.
- Ask the buyer to check the assumptions and to discuss approval, data and handover of the result.
- Explain the price through scope and costs, without artificial urgency.
- Write down the agreed next steps. If needed, remind them once, respecting a refusal.

Play a potential buyer of [pilot] in [fictional situation]. Ask about anything unclear in the scope, price and data handling. At the end, separately evaluate my explanation and list the facts that still need to be verified.

Check: the written offer matches the actual agreements. Objections from the role-play are not presented as the words of real clients.
