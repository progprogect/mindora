## Your practical result

A short, organised view of what is on your mind and one action you choose to take. This is a planning exercise, not a diagnosis or a promise to remove distress.

A hands-on guide with a reusable prompt, a worked example and a short practice task. Use it at your own pace.

## A simple route through the task

Everything on your mind → Four manageable groups → One chosen next step

## Have these ready

-   A rough list of tasks and concerns
-   Actual deadlines and commitments
-   Time available right now
-   Your current energy and the amount of help you want

## Your reusable assistant prompt

The block contains the full prompt. Select it and copy; replace only the bracketed fields.

Help me organise my thoughts without judging or overwhelming me. First ask me to write what is on my mind in any order. Reflect it back briefly and ask which items are actual tasks, fixed commitments or concerns outside my control. Separate: act now, schedule, waiting for someone, and no action available now. Do not invent deadlines or turn every concern into a task. Ask me to choose one useful next action that fits \[TIME\] and my current energy. Give only that action, then wait. Do not diagnose, offer treatment or promise to remove anxiety.

## The workflow, step by step

1

### Empty the list without sorting

Write fragments, not a polished explanation. Avoid sharing other people's private information. You can use neutral descriptions such as 'reply to colleague' instead of names and sensitive details.

2

### Name what each item is

A task can become a concrete action. A concern may need a question, a conversation or no action right now. Ask the assistant to check rather than assigning urgency to everything.

3

### Confirm the real deadlines

Mark dates as fixed, preferred or unknown. Do not let the assistant invent a deadline because a task sounds important. Decide what consequences matter today.

4

### Choose the smallest useful action

Use a verb and an object: 'find the appointment phone number' is clearer than 'sort out health'. Fit the action to your available time and energy. You may choose a different action from the assistant's suggestion.

5

### Close the loop

After finishing, mark it done and save a short note: what changed, what remains and the next review time you selected. If you are done for now, say so; a helpful assistant should respect that stopping point.

## When the answer is not useful

### It gives me too many tasks

Show only one action that takes no more than \[TIME\]. Keep the rest in a later list.

### It invents urgency

Separate confirmed deadlines from assumptions. Ask me before assigning a date.

### It diagnoses how I feel

Remove diagnostic language. Help me organise the list and choose a practical next step.

### It makes a promise for me

Do not invent commitments in a message. Ask what deadline I can genuinely meet.

## Try it with your own information

Spend two minutes listing what is on your mind. Sort it into the four groups, verify deadlines and choose one action for the next five to ten minutes. Finish by recording what you did. Success is a clearer decision, not completing every item.
